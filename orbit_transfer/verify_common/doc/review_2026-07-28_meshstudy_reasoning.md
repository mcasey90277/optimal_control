# Reasoning review of the mesh/basin study (2026-07-28)

Two independent reviews of `mesh_and_basin_study.tex` + the raw running record,
scoped to REASONING (inferences, alternative explanations), not prose or layout.
Both independently identified the same fatal flaw and named it the weakest link:
the 1 N sliding windows were computed ACROSS a 173 -> 171 switch-count change,
violating the study's own stated Richardson restriction.

---

## GPT-5.6-terra

- **[CORRECTNESS]** `Claim 1: p = 1.228 supports H1 over H2` -- **Does not survive as stated.** The only branch-consistent window is \(2\times\!\to4\times\!\to8\times\): 171 switches and \(p=1.228\). One three-level ratio is an order *estimate*, not evidence of an asymptotic order or a trend. The preceding \(p=1.596\) uses the 173-switch \(1\times\) result and is not commensurable with the 171-switch branch; calling the sequence “decreasing toward 1” is therefore unjustified. Even if it were one branch, two effective orders decreasing from 1.596 to 1.228 can arise from pre-asymptotic mixed terms and does not exclude eventual second order. The sound claim is: “On the 171-switch branch, the final three available levels give a local effective order 1.228, below 2 but insufficient to distinguish asymptotic first from second order.” Continue that *same branch* through at least \(16\times\), preferably \(32\times\), and test whether successive fine-window orders stabilize near 1 or 2. Fit competing \(f_0+C_1h+C_2h^2\) and \(f_0+C_2h^2+C_3h^3\) models only with enough independent levels to assess residuals.

- **[CORRECTNESS]** `Claim 2: 1.21 kg basin error and 0.35 kg discretization error at 1 N` -- **Does not survive.** The \(1372.156\) kg extrapolate belongs, if it belongs anywhere, to the 171-switch \(2\times/4\times/8\times\) ladder branch. The production winner is a 177-switch solution at \(1\times\); the production ladder point is 173 switches; and the cached certified value is yet another solve-path result. Subtracting \(1371.811\) from an extrapolated 171-switch limit is not a mesh-error estimate for the 177-switch production solution. Likewise, \(1.553-0.345=1.208\) kg is arithmetic, not an identifiable basin/discretization decomposition. The sound claim is only that a substantially better feasible stationary point was found on the production NLP, and that the 171-switch fine-ladder branch appears to settle. To estimate production-mesh error, warm-continue the 177-switch best production solution to \(2\times,4\times,8\times,\ldots\), verify branch identity with more than switch count, and extrapolate that branch. Do the analogous continuation for the 171- and 173-switch branches if branch-dependent mesh error is itself of interest.

- **[CORRECTNESS]** `Claim 3: basin selection outweighs discretization error in every row` -- **Does not survive as a quantitative error-budget conclusion.** A fixed-grid improvement between stationary points is a demonstrated *search/basin opportunity*. A Richardson residual, when valid, is an estimate of approximation error along one branch. They are useful operational quantities, but they are neither the same error nor presently measured on matched branches. Thus the \(100\times\) ratio at 10 N rests on the invalid 0.009 kg “within-branch” estimate: its two values have 19 and 20 switches. The \(4\times\) ratio at 1 N rests on the invalid 0.345 kg cross-branch subtraction. The sound practical claim is narrower and important: “For all three Earth production NLPs, the seed set found fixed-grid improvements of 1.015–5.971 kg, so basin search is an immediately higher-yield intervention than blindly accepting the cached solve.” It becomes a defensible dominance statement only after matched-branch refinement ladders provide branch-specific mesh residuals.

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `10 N residual mesh error = 0.009 kg` -- The 193- to 386-node change is not a within-branch mesh measurement: switch count changes 19 to 20, and no branch-continuation evidence is supplied. Moreover, the purported 18-switch branch has \(1378.117\) kg at 193 nodes and \(1377.875\) kg at 1544 nodes, a 0.242 kg difference of the opposite sign. Two levels cannot establish either convergence or an error bound, but they directly show that 0.009 kg is not a general mesh-adequacy measure for the relevant alternative branch. Refine the coarse 18-switch optimum through multiple meshes and separately refine the 19/20-switch family.

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `Claim 4: certified rows are pessimistic by 1.0–6.0 kg` -- **Partly survives, but not at the stated certainty.** The fixed-grid higher-mass solutions are meaningful evidence that the listed cached solutions were not the best stationary points found for their respective NLPs, assuming identical physical constraints and independently reconstructed feasibility. But they do not establish global optimality, nor do they establish a reproducible lower bound of 1.0–6.0 kg under a defined certification protocol. At 2.5 N, the 0.505 kg spread caused by nominally trivial seed handling shows the protocol is not stable enough to treat a single cache as a reference value. The phrase “a \(10^{-12}\) seed change” is especially unsupported unless norms before and after normalization/clipping, active-set changes, and resulting control differences are reported; projection onto unit-direction and box constraints can amplify a small raw perturbation into a structurally meaningful solver-path change.

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `Is the 10 N 1.015 kg gain above the noise floor?` -- Exact reproduction of the 10 N baseline is good evidence that *that exact solve protocol* is locally deterministic, and it makes a 1.015 kg difference very unlikely to be floating-point objective-evaluation noise. It does not by itself define a per-row solve-path variability floor. More importantly, “noise” conflates two distinct issues: objective/feasibility numerical error, which should be near solver tolerances, and nonconvex basin sensitivity, which is the phenomenon under study. The correct claim is: “Under a repeatable baseline protocol, an independently seeded feasible solution is 1.015 kg better.” Establish a row-specific robustness statement by repeating both baseline and winning seeds under controlled perturbations, IPOPT barrier initializations, linear-solver settings, and preprocessing variants; report distributions or at least worst/best values. The 2.5 N behavior is not evidence that the 10 N gain is noise, but it prevents applying one row’s reproducibility assessment to another.

- **[CORRECTNESS]** `Sections 4.2 / 5.3: down-projection proves the 8x rung escaped a basin rather than resolved coarse-mesh limitations` -- **Only partly survives.** The test does prove an important, narrower fact: the 193-node NLP admits an 18-switch stationary solution with final mass \(1378.117\) kg, better than the cached 19-switch one. It therefore rejects the claim that the coarse NLP cannot represent *any useful version* of that structure. It does **not** prove why the 8x solve found it. Fine resolution may alter the discrete landscape, create a continuation path across a basin boundary, better resolve switch placement before projection, or simply generate an unusually effective structured seed. Nor does it prove the 18-switch structures at 193 and 1544 nodes approximate the same continuous extremal. Call it a successful cross-mesh basin-seeding experiment, not a clean causal discriminator between basin escape and resolution effects.

- **[SCOPE]** `New tulip null probe` -- Four seeds returning \(m_f=0.84906583\), 25 switches, and agreement to \(10^{-12}\) establish only invariance under those four nearby throttle-shift perturbations and the specified solver path. This is useful positive evidence of local robustness, stronger than nothing, but it does not support “robustly optimal,” “best basin,” or even robustness to the seed class that won every Earth comparison. Equality to \(10^{-12}\) is also a solver-output agreement, not an estimate of continuous-problem error. A stronger conclusion requires a mesh ladder; down-projection of each fine result onto 4001 nodes; continuation of promising production solutions back upward; substantially broader structured switch-time/arc insertion-deletion perturbations; alternate homotopy and barrier paths; and, ideally, an independently formulated or independently integrated validation.

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `Switch count as branch evidence` -- The document correctly says count is not a reliable branch identifier, but subsequently treats stable counts as enough to call the 1 N fine ladder one branch. Equal count does not establish equal switch ordering, switch locations, thrust directions, or multiplier structure; the 2.5 N result is the study’s own counterexample. Branch identity should be assessed with matched switch times, arc correspondence, controls/costates where available, and continuation from a common seed. The falling matched-count diagnostic is also confounded because its matching window shrinks with mesh; it is not a direct convergence statistic without normalizing displacement against a common physical or independent-variable scale.

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `“Machine-tight defects” and KKT checks establish physical fidelity` -- They establish accurate solution of each transcription, not adequacy of trapezoidal propagation between nodes. This distinction is stated early but weakened later when “genuine converged optimum” is used for a discrete stationary point. For bang-bang trajectories, independent high-accuracy integration of the reconstructed control, endpoint mismatch under that integration, interval-residual/error indicators, and sensitivity to switch localization are needed to support continuous-trajectory claims. AD derivatives remove derivative approximation error; they do not remove transcription, quadrature, discontinuity-location, or local-optimum error.

- **[SCOPE]** `“The mesh was not the problem” / “production meshes are broadly adequate”` -- The evidence does not support this breadth. Two of three ladders failed the study’s own convergence criterion. The only apparently settling fine ladder starts at \(2\times\), not at the production branch, and its production cache/ladder/best-seed values differ by 0.25–1.45 kg. The supported observation is that coarse meshes can contain substantially better solutions than the cached ones; that is not an estimate of their continuous approximation error.

**VERDICT:** (a) **NOT SUPPORTED**: no branch-matched production-mesh convergence estimate has been established for any of the three rows. (b) **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** operationally: fixed-mesh basin/seed choice demonstrably changes the best-found Earth solutions by kilograms, but its claimed dominance over discretization is not quantitatively established. The single weakest link is treating Richardson quantities from one branch as mesh-error bars for different production branches.

## Gemini 3.1 Pro

- **[CORRECTNESS]** `Claim 1: The observed order p = 1.228 at the 1 N row supports hypothesis H1 (p ~ 1) over H2 (p ~ 2)` -- The inference is fatally flawed because the first window calculation explicitly violates your own axiom in Section 3.3 ("Forbidden: anything measured across a branch change"). Your switch counts for 1 N are 173 / 171 / 171 / 171. The first difference (+0.984) is a jump from 173 to 171 switches. Therefore, the first sliding window ($p=1.596$) is mathematically meaningless. You only have *one* valid sliding window ($p=1.228$, from the 2x/4x/8x levels). Without a second valid window, you cannot claim the sequence is "trending" or has reached an asymptotic regime at all. To make the claim sound, you must run a 16x mesh, calculate the 4x/8x/16x window, and verify if it stabilizes near 1.0 or diverges.

- **[CORRECTNESS]** `Claim 2: Of the 1.55 kg gap... ~1.21 kg is basin error and only ~0.35 kg is discretization` -- This decomposition is numerically invalid. You are subtracting quantities defined on different topological branches. The Richardson limit ($1372.156$ kg) is extrapolated from the 171-switch branch. You immediately subtract it from the production-mesh baseline of the *177-switch* branch. Subtracting the continuous limit of Branch A from the discrete evaluation of Branch B does not isolate the discretization error of Branch B; it yields an unphysical hybrid of truncation error and topological distance. To legally decompose this gap, you must run a new mesh ladder explicitly seeded to hold the 177-switch structure, find its unique Richardson limit, and compute the differences within that single branch.

- **[SCOPE]** `Claim 3: Basin selection outweighs discretization error in every row examined` -- Placing these in a direct ratio ("~100x at 10 N") is a categorical error. A topology change is a discrete, algorithmic failure of global search; mesh convergence is the continuous truncation error of the integration scheme. They scale differently and represent different phenomena. However, measured strictly in terms of *unrealized payload mass at fixed compute budget*, the practical conclusion stands perfectly. To make the claim rigorous, abandon the mathematical ratios and state it as an payload/engineering bound: "Resolving the optimal branch yields an order of magnitude more payload mass than refining a suboptimal branch." 

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `Claim 4: The certified rows are pessimistic by 1.0-6.0 kg` -- The claim survives for 10 N, but the evidence needs tightening regarding the "noise floor". Because the 10 N baseline reproduces exactly (+0.000000 kg) when self-seeded, its local minimum is mathematically crisp. A +1.015 kg gain combined with a topology shift (19 to 18 switches) is definitively a new basin, safely evading any noise floor. However, your 2.5 N row exhibits 0.5 kg of variation under $10^{-12}$ seed perturbations. In a barrier method NLP, this extreme sensitivity usually signifies that the solver is hopping between dozens of hyper-shallow sub-basins, or the KKT matrix is severely ill-conditioned near bang-bang transitions. A +5.971 kg jump easily clears the 0.5 kg threshold, meaning the pessimism claim holds, but you must clarify that your "noise" is actually numerical instability / sub-basin hopping driven by the regularization scheme, not floating-point rounding. 

- **[MINOR]** `Claim 5: Down-projection concludes the 8x rung escaped a basin rather than resolved something the coarse mesh could not represent` -- The logic here is sound. If an 18-switch solution solves the production grid's NLP to established defect tolerances ($< 10^{-14}$) and yields a higher objective, then the coarse grid was fundamentally capable of representing that superior basin all along. The original failure was pure algorithm search trajectory, not discretization capacity. The only caveat (which does not derail the conclusion) is that the *continuous* 18-switch optimum might still require a fine grid to achieve its theoretical maximum mass, but you have successfully proven the coarse mesh NLP was artificially leaving mass on the table due to basin trapping.

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `Claim 6: Flagship probe null result (4001 nodes, 25 switches) survives four different seeds` -- Concluding robust optimality from this null result is unsupported because the adversarial seeds applied here are structurally weak. In your Earth rows, the catastrophic failures were induced by *down-projected seeds* from 4x/8x meshes. Fine grids resolve brief transient boundary arcs that coarse grids suppress; projecting these back down radically reorganizes the NLP gradients. Shifting throttle profiles by $\pm 1$ or $\pm 8$ nodes is merely a localized perturbation that easily falls back into the parent basin. To conclude this row is robustly optimal, it must survive the same adversary: you must run a cislunar mesh ladder, down-project it, and verify the flagship resists falling into a newly discovered topology. 

- **[ROBUSTNESS]** `Claim 7: "the value this particular solve path happened to reach" (Section 5.4, observation 3)` -- You note that re-unitizing the thrust direction vector and clipping the throttle box on the exact same row yields a 0.5 kg difference on the 2.5 N solution. If the NLP solver legitimately arrives at two distinct points separated by 0.5 kg under exact boundary conditions just from a trivial NLP variable rescale, your interior point tolerance metrics are functionally meaningless for the cost dimension. This implies your NLP is effectively flat over a 0.5 kg domain. You need to inspect the condition number of the Hessian of the Lagrangian at convergence; if the problem is flat, standard certification checks are blind to it. 

VERDICT:
(a) The claim that production meshes are broadly adequate is **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**. The evidence is directionally plausible, but the sole mathematical proof of convergence (the 1 N order calculation) is invalid due to a topology-crossing error.
(b) The claim that basin selection is the dominant error source is **SUPPORTED**. Even if comparing continuum errors to discrete jumps is a formal category error, down-projections undisputedly prove that massive payload gains exist on the *exact same unmodified meshes* simply by altering the optimizer's starting guess. 

**Single weakest link:** Computing Richardson extrapolations and sliding windows across a 173-to-171 switch branch transition for the 1 N case. This blatantly violates the study's own rules for mesh convergence and renders the 1.55 kg gap decomposition mathematically fictitious.
