Lunar PNT: Minimum-Time Low-Thrust Transfers from a Distant Retrograde Orbit
to Lunar Tulip Orbits

Tulip-shaped cislunar orbits offer superior navigation geometry over the Moon's
southern hemisphere, where sustained surface operations are planned.  A
constellation in such orbits is only useful if its satellites can be delivered
there affordably, so we address the deployment problem: moving a navigation and
relay satellite from a distant retrograde orbit, a stable and accessible staging
point in cislunar space, into its operational tulip orbit on continuous low
thrust, in the shortest possible time.

We model a 150 kg spacecraft in the Earth-Moon circular restricted three-body
problem, propelled by a Busek BHT-1500 Hall thruster at its published operating
point of 101 mN and 1710 s specific impulse. Both the departure orbit and the
target are periodic solutions of that model: a distant retrograde orbit about
the Moon, and a seven-petal tulip orbit whose apoapses hang over the southern
hemisphere.

The transfer is solved by indirect optimal control: Pontryagin's minimum
principle converts the minimum-time problem into a two-point boundary value
problem, with the thrust direction given exactly by the primer vector and the
magnitude at its upper bound throughout. The resulting trajectory satisfies the
first-order necessary conditions of optimality. The numerical methods are
informed by the indirect approach of Zhang, Topputo, Bernelli-Zazzera and Zhao
("Low-Thrust Minimum-Fuel Optimization in the Circular Restricted Three-Body
Problem," Journal of Guidance, Control, and Dynamics, 2015,
doi:10.2514/1.G001080), who solved the analogous low-thrust transfer problem in
the same dynamical model.

The minimum-time transfer takes 12.1 days. It requires 0.72 km/s of delta-V and
6.3 kg of propellant, roughly four percent of the wet mass, at an average thrust
acceleration near 0.07 cm/s squared.

A constellation follows from the same trajectory. Every satellite flies an
identical transfer, so a staggered schedule of departures from the shared
staging orbit delivers all of them onto the tulip orbit. We adopt the optimized
phasing derived in our recent work on tulip-orbit constellations for lunar
south-pole navigation and direct-to-Earth relay (arXiv:2607.10482), so the
deployment places each spacecraft directly into its operational slot. Deployment
therefore requires one trajectory design and a release schedule rather than a
separate optimization for each spacecraft, and a replacement satellite launched
later reaches station on the same 12.1-day arc.

These results show that a southern-hemisphere PNT constellation can be deployed
from a single cislunar staging orbit using flight-qualified electric propulsion,
with each satellite reaching its station in under two weeks.
