Lunar PNT: Minimum-Time Low-Thrust Transfers from a Distant Retrograde Orbit
to Lunar Tulip Orbits

Tulip-shaped cislunar orbits offer superior navigation geometry over the Moon's
southern hemisphere, where sustained surface operations are planned.  A constellation
in such orbits is only useful if its satellites can be delivered there
affordably, so we address the deployment problem: moving a navigation and relay
satellite from a distant retrograde orbit, a stable and accessible staging point
in cislunar space, into its operational tulip orbit on continuous low thrust, in
the shortest possible time.

We model a 150 kg spacecraft with 70 mN of electric propulsion and a specific
impulse of 900 seconds, flying in the Earth-Moon circular restricted three-body
problem. Both the departure orbit and the target are periodic solutions of that
model: a distant retrograde orbit about the Moon, and a seven-petal tulip orbit
whose apoapses hang over the southern hemisphere.

The transfer is solved by indirect optimal control: Pontryagin's minimum
principle turns the minimum-time problem into a two-point boundary value
problem in which the thrust direction is given exactly by the primer vector and
the thrust magnitude sits at its upper bound throughout.  The resulting trajectory satisfies the first-order necessary
conditions of optimality. The numerical methods are informed by the indirect
approach of Zhang, Topputo, Bernelli-Zazzera and Zhao ("Low-Thrust Minimum-Fuel
Optimization in the Circular Restricted Three-Body Problem," Journal of
Guidance, Control, and Dynamics, 2015, doi:10.2514/1.G001080), who solved the
analogous low-thrust transfer problem in the same dynamical model.

The minimum-time transfer takes about 17.8 days. It requires 0.75 km/s of
delta-V and 12.2 kg of propellant, roughly eight percent of the wet mass, at an
average thrust acceleration near 0.05 cm/s squared. The trajectory spirals away
from the distant retrograde orbit under continuous thrust and is captured
directly onto the tulip, with no coast phase and no impulsive maneuver anywhere
in the sequence.

A constellation follows from the same trajectory. Every satellite flies an
identical transfer, so a staggered schedule of departures from the shared
staging orbit delivers all of them onto the tulip orbit.

We adopt the optimized phasing
derived in our recent work on tulip-orbit constellations for lunar south-pole
navigation and direct-to-Earth relay (arXiv:2607.10482), so the deployment
places each spacecraft directly into its operational slot. Deployment therefore
requires one trajectory design and a release schedule rather than a separate
optimization for each spacecraft, and a replacement satellite launched later
reaches station on the same 17.8-day arc.

These results show that a southern-hemisphere PNT constellation can be deployed
from a single cislunar staging orbit using low thrust, with each satellite
reaching its station in under three weeks.
