01 · The Problem
A tiny engine, a long trip, and one question: when do I fire?

Our spacecraft leaves a Distant Retrograde Orbit around the Moon and has to arrive on a tulip orbit at a fixed time. Its engine is a weak ion thruster: 0.07 N pushing 150 kg. Think of blowing on a bowling ball.

The only decisions are: which way to point the thruster and how hard to run it, every instant, for 20–30 days.

"Which way" turns out to be easy: point against the so-called primer vector. The whole difficulty is "how hard".

ELI5

You are pushing a shopping cart across a huge, bumpy parking lot to a spot you must reach by 5 pm. You may push as gently or as hard as you like at any moment. Use the least total effort.

01 · The Problem
Minimum fuel wants the engine ON or OFF, never in between

Fuel used is proportional to how hard you run the engine, summed over the trip: $J = \int_0^{t_f} s\,dt$ with throttle $s \in [0,1]$. Because that cost is a straight line in $s$, the best plan never uses half-throttle: it is bang-bang — full burn when firing helps enough, coast otherwise.

The switch rule (Pontryagin)

A single number $Q(t)$ — built from the costates, our "shadow prices" — decides everything:

$$ s^*(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & Q(t) > 1 \\ 0 & Q(t) < 1 \end{cases} \qquad Q = T\left(\frac{\|\lambda_v\|}{m} + \frac{\lambda_m}{c}\right) $$
Why this is hard for a computer

A step function has no slope. Newton's method — the workhorse behind every solver we use — steers by slopes. Aim it at a bang-bang problem cold and it has nothing to hold on to.

02 · Melting It
The trick: solve an easy, smooth problem first, then sharpen it step by step

Replace the fuel cost by a softened cost with a dial $p$. At $p = 1$ the problem is smooth and friendly (minimum energy, $\int s^2\,dt$). As $p \to 0$ it becomes exactly minimum fuel.

Solve at $p = 1$. Nudge $p$ down. Use the last answer as the guess for the next. Repeat down a ladder of 17 rungs to $p = 0.001$.

This is a homotopy, or continuation. Each rung is a small step for Newton.

ELI5

You cannot carve a sharp corner in frozen butter. So you warm the butter, shape it roughly, and put it back in the fridge a little at a time, tidying the corner as it firms up. The dial $p$ is the fridge temperature.

02 · Melting It
Two ways to soften a step: the ε ramp and the Huber knee

ε (Bertrand–Epenoy) — cost $(1-p)s + p s^2$. Throttle is a ramp from 0 to 1 across $Q \in (1-p,\,1+p)$. Continuous. Coasts exactly when $Q < 1-p$.

Huber (PLQ) — cost $s^2/2p$ below the knee, linear above. Throttle $s = pQ$ up to $Q = 1$, then jumps to 1. Never coasts exactly: $s = pQ > 0$.

Both become bang-bang as $p \to 0$. Which walks the ladder better was the "race" of 2 September.

The mechanism we wrote down

"Huber's throttle jumps at the switch, so Newton fights a residual floor it can never get under. The jump is the problem." It sounded right. It was wrong.

03 · The Bug
Newton needs to know how the end of the trip moves when the start wiggles

That sensitivity is the state transition matrix $\Phi$. We get it by integrating $\dot\Phi = A\,\Phi$ alongside the trajectory, where $A$ is the local slope of the dynamics.

ELI5

You roll a marble down a ramp and want to know how far the landing spot shifts if you nudge the start. On a smooth ramp: easy, follow the slope. But if the ramp has a step in it, the marble flies off the edge — and a nudge at the start changes when it reaches the edge, which changes the landing spot a lot. Following the local slope alone misses that.

The Huber law has a step

At $Q = 1$ the throttle jumps from $pQ$ to 1. Our propagator integrated $\dot\Phi = A\Phi$ straight through it — local slope only. The switching-time sensitivity was missing.

03 · The Bug
The fix: a "saltation" (jump) matrix at every crossing
What the STM must do at a switch surface $Q(y) = 1$
$$ \Phi^{+} \;=\; \Big[\, I \;+\; \frac{(F^{+} - F^{-})\,\nabla Q^{\mathsf T}}{\nabla Q^{\mathsf T} F^{-}} \Big]\, \Phi^{-} $$

$F^-$, $F^+$ are the dynamics just before and after the jump; $\nabla Q$ points across the switch surface. The bracket is how much a change in arrival time at the step moves the state.

Implementation: integrate each smooth branch with the law pinned, detect the crossing with an event function, apply the matrix, restart on the other branch. The ε ramp is continuous ($F^+ = F^-$), so it needs none of this — which is why ε was never affected.

What the "residual floor" really was

Newton, handed a wrong map, walking in circles. The floor vanished the moment the map was right. Two arms now land on the same final mass to $4.5 \times 10^{-6}$ — the same solution, reached six times faster.

04 · Results
Two families, two personalities
ε ramp — slow, but always arrives

Reached the floor on 7 of 7 cells. Failed 8–10 times per cell, every time recovered by bisecting the step. 6–12 minutes each.

Huber — fast, or walls

Reached the floor on 4 of 7 cells with 0–1 failures in 1–2 minutes, landing on the identical solution. On the other three it hit a hard residual floor and retired.

ELI5

ε is the friend who drives the speed limit and always gets there. Huber is the friend who takes the back roads — usually much faster, occasionally stuck behind a closed bridge with no way round.

04 · Results
A control that paid off: the cold start was our seed, not Huber

Both arms start at $p = 1$ from the min-energy solution. For ε that is the energy problem. For Huber at $p = 1$ the cost is $s^2/2$, whose minimizer is $s^* = Q$ — not the energy law's $s^* = Q/2$. Same trajectory, but Huber wants half the costates.

05 · Why
Why Huber walks cleaner where it works (hypothesis)

The ε ramp's slope is $1/2p$. At $p = 0.001$ that is 500: a near-vertical ramp Newton must balance on at every switch. Its "failures" are Newton overshooting that ramp and the ladder bisecting to a gentler step.

Huber's core is $s = pQ$, slope $p$ — gentle and getting gentler as $p \to 0$. The sharpness lives entirely in the jump, which the saltation matrix now handles exactly. There is nothing steep left for Newton to slip on.

ELI5

ε hides the cliff by turning it into a very steep hill; you still have to climb it carefully. Huber keeps the cliff but tells you exactly where it is and how to step off it. Once you trust the map, the cliff is the easier route.

05 · Why
Why it walls where it walls (hypothesis)

Huber never coasts exactly: wherever $Q > 0$, $s = pQ > 0$. On a cell whose true fuel solution is mostly coast, Huber must represent long near-zero arcs with a tiny positive throttle, and $Q$ hovers near the jump at 1 for long stretches.

Suspect, not convicted

A near-grazing crossing — $Q$ touching 1 without crossing cleanly — makes the saltation denominator $\nabla Q^{\mathsf T}F^-$ tiny and the jump matrix huge. That would produce exactly the residual floors we see. The correlation with coast fraction is suggestive, not monotone. The mechanism study would settle it.

06 · How To Use It
Idea 1: the hedged race

Run both families from the same energy seed (Huber on $\lambda/2$). Take whichever reaches the floor first. Keep ε as the fallback that always arrives.

Expected payoff on this grid

4 of 7 cells finish in 1–2 min instead of 6–11; the other 3 cost one extra failed Huber attempt (~2–7 min) before ε runs as usual. Net: roughly half the wall time for a catalog build, same answers.

ELI5

Send both friends. Whoever texts "I'm here" first wins; the other turns around. You never arrive later than the reliable friend alone.

06 · How To Use It
Idea 2: the handoff at the wall

Both families converge to the same bang-bang problem as $p \to 0$. So when Huber walls at, say, $p = 0.033$, its junction states are already an excellent seed for ε at a nearby $p$ — deep in the ladder, past ε's own slow early rungs.

% sketch: Huber fast to the wall, epsilon from there
[zH, itH] = walk('huber', seedEnergyHalf, sched); % stops at pWall or floor
if itH.pDeepest > pFloor
  seedE = itH.Y; % same junctions, same limit problem
  [zE, itE] = walk('eps', seedE, sched(sched < itH.pDeepest));
end

Unbuilt. The two families' $p$ values are not the same "temperature", so the handoff $p$ needs one calibration experiment.

06 · How To Use It
Idea 3: send Huber where ε could not go

The real test of a "new line of attack" is not the cells ε already solves. It is the cells it cannot enter:

  • the high-γ band (long flight times) where the direct min-energy solve itself fails;
  • the deep-thrust wall at 0.09 → 0.067 N (a basin wall, ~1.3 revolutions);
  • the GTO → tulip 1.01–1.11 × tf,min band, open since July.
Why there is hope

ε's trouble on hard cells is the steep ramp at small $p$ — exactly the thing Huber does not have. Whether Huber's own weakness (long coasts) bites on those cells is unknown; the deep-thrust cells are all-burn-ish, which is Huber's good regime.

06 · How To Use It
Idea 4: Huber is one member of a family (PLQ)

Huber is the simplest piecewise linear-quadratic (PLQ) penalty in Rockafellar's sense — quadratic near zero, linear far away. Our mlabTools optim/ already ships the general class (plq_make, plq_penalty).

Knobs we have not turned

  • the knee position and a dead zone (a PLQ that does coast exactly — fixing Huber's one structural weakness);
  • asymmetric slopes on the two sides of the knee;
  • PLQ in the direct NLP objective, not just the indirect throttle law.

ELI5

Huber is one recipe for softening the step. PLQ is the whole cookbook. We tried one page and it worked; the page about "soft step with an exact off-switch" is the one to try next.

07 · Lessons
Three things worth remembering
1 · A wrong Jacobian looks exactly like a structural wall

Newton stalling at a residual floor is what you see when the problem is hard and when your map is wrong. Before declaring a method "structurally unsuited", check the map against finite differences. Ten lines; it would have saved a wrong conclusion.

2 · Discontinuous control needs saltation, everywhere

Any sensitivity, first- or second-order, that passes through a switch without the jump matrix is wrong. This applies to the conjugate-point test and to the future switching-time Hessian too.

3 · Test the seed, not just the method

Two of seven "Huber failures" were a cold seed. The $\lambda/2$ fix cost nothing and came from someone reading the Hamiltonian, not running code.

Next steps, in order of value per hour

  2. Mechanism study on (6,8)@1.2 and (1,2): count the $Q = 1$ crossings per segment, log $\nabla Q^{\mathsf T}F^-$ at each, test the grazing hypothesis. Decides whether the wall is fixable.
  3. Hedged race + handoff in the catalog builder, λ/2 as Huber's default seed. Halves catalog wall time if the 4-of-7 rate holds.
